\subsection{Detection performance on Elliptic}
\label{subsec:main}

RTXGNN reaches a test F1 of \rtxFone{}\,$\pm$\,\rtxFoneSd{} and an AP of \rtxAP{}. Among the twelve graph neural networks, only GAS obtains a practically identical F1 (\gasFone), and FRAUDRE and GAS obtain a practically identical AP (\fraudreAP{} and \gasAP). RTXGNN is ahead of all other GNNs on F1, including the general GNNs (GraphSAGE \sageFone, GCN \gcnFone, GAT \gatFone), the temporal GNNs (TGN \tgnFone, TGAT \tgatFone) and the fraud-specific models. In particular, RTXGNN improves on the other self-explaining model, SEFraud, by \deltaFOnesefraud{} F1 ($p=\prawFOnesefraud$, uncorrected) and \deltaAPsefraud{} AP ($p=\prawAPsefraud$). After Holm correction over the 16 comparisons, the F1 differences to the GNN baselines are no longer significant except for PC-GNN, while the AP differences to GCN, GAT, CARE-GNN and PC-GNN remain significant (Table~\ref{tab:main}).

Two feature-only models remain strong competitors on Elliptic's engineered features. XGBoost obtains the highest F1 (\xgbFone) and AP (\xgbAP), significantly above RTXGNN. The random forest reaches F1 \rfFone{} and the same AP as RTXGNN (\rfAP), but its F1 varies considerably with the seed-dependent threshold ($\pm$0.05). The strength of tree ensembles on these features agrees with \citet{weber2019anti} and with the general finding of \citet{grinsztajn2022tree}. The Elliptic features already contain 72 aggregates of the one-hop neighbourhood, so much of the graph information is available to a feature-only model. The trees do not produce explanations over the relations between transactions, which is the purpose of RTXGNN. The MLP, which sees the same features without message passing, is \deltaFOnemlp{} F1 below RTXGNN.

EvolveGCN-O is the weakest graph model (\evolvegcnFone). Its recurrent weight evolution has to be carried from the training period through all test snapshots without supervision. Because no transaction appears in more than one snapshot, the model cannot exploit the node-level continuity for which it was designed. The continuous-time models TGN and TGAT perform similarly to static GNNs, for the reasons discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}.

An F1 around 0.7 still means that errors are frequent. At the validation-chosen threshold, RTXGNN raises on average \rtxAlerts{} alerts in the test period, misses \rtxMissed{} of the \nTestIllicit{} illicit transactions (\rtxMissPct\%), and \rtxFalse{} of its alerts (\rtxFDRPct\%) concern licit transactions (Table~\ref{tab:operational}). As a stand-alone decision rule this would not be acceptable. The ranking, however, is highly informative for review queues with limited capacity: among the 500 highest-scored test transactions, \rtxPrecAtFive{} of those flagged by RTXGNN are illicit, and among the top 1,000 the proportion is \rtxPrecAtThousand. The differences between the strongest models appear mainly at the level of the automatic threshold, where XGBoost produces fewer false alerts. For a decision maker, the relevant choice is therefore the size of the review queue. The threshold should be set by the capacity of the investigation team and by the relative cost of a missed case and a false alert, not by maximising F1.

\subsection{Temporal behaviour, drift and retraining}
\label{subsec:temporal}

\begin{table}[!t]

\label{tab:temporal}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3.5pt}
\input{tables/temporal.tex}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/temporal.pdf}
\caption{F1 per test time step (lines; shaded band: $\pm$1 s.d.\ over seeds) and number of illicit transactions per step (grey bars, right axis). Dashed lines: models retrained before every step with a one-step label delay.}
\label{fig:temporal}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:temporal} and Figure~\ref{fig:temporal} break the test period down by time step. The period has two clearly different phases. In steps 35--42, RTXGNN reaches a per-step F1 between 0.66 and 0.94 (mean \rtxEarlyFone), with recall above 0.8 in most steps, and the random forest behaves very similarly. From step 43 onwards all models fail: the mean per-step F1 of RTXGNN over steps 43--49 is \rtxLateFone{}, and that of the random forest is \rfStaticLateFone. \citet{weber2019anti} attribute this collapse to the shutdown of a large dark market after step 42. At the same time, the number of illicit transactions per step falls from between 33 and 239 to between 2 and 56, so the late steps carry only \illLate{} of the \nTestIllicit{} illicit test transactions (\illLatePct\%).

This explains why a near-zero F1 in the late steps is compatible with a pooled test F1 of about 0.7. The pooled F1 is computed once over all test transactions and is therefore dominated by the \illEarly{} illicit transactions of steps 35--42. The unweighted mean of the fifteen per-step F1 scores of RTXGNN is only \rtxMeanStepFone.

\paragraph{Retraining with a one-step label delay} To assess how much of the collapse a deployment could recover, we refit the models before every test step $t$ on all labelled steps up to $t-5$, validate and choose the threshold on steps $t-4$ to $t-1$, and score step $t$. Labels thus become available one step after the transactions occur. Neural models are warm-started from the previous step's model. Retraining improves the early steps slightly (RTXGNN \rtxgnnRetrEarlyFone, random forest \rfRetrEarlyFone). In the late steps it raises the mean F1 of RTXGNN from \rtxgnnStaticLateFone{} to \rtxgnnRetrLateFone{} and that of the random forest from \rfStaticLateFone{} to \rfRetrLateFone. The recovery is real but partial. Once the illicit activity changes, a model can only learn the new pattern after labelled examples of it exist, and the late steps contain very few of them. Retraining is therefore necessary but not sufficient, and a deployment also needs drift monitoring and complementary controls (Section~\ref{subsec:deployment}).

\begin{figure}[!t]

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fidelity.pdf}
\caption{Sparsity--fidelity trade-off of feature explanations on Elliptic (mean over 5 models and 600 test transactions).}
\label{fig:fidelity}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:explanations} and Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity} compare the feature explanations of 600 test transactions (300 illicit, 300 licit) for five independently trained models. The post-hoc explainers are applied to the same RTXGNN models, so differences reflect the explainers, not the predictors.

\paragraph{Sufficiency} Keeping only the ten features selected by SEAL changes the predicted-class probability by \fidMmask{} on average (Fid$^-$@10). This is better than saliency (\fidMsaliency) and close to Integrated Gradients (\fidMig), and it is far better than a random selection (\fidMrandom) and than SEFraud's masks on SEFraud (\fidMsefraudmask). The sufficiency term is responsible for this property. The same architecture trained without $\mathcal{L}_{suf}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{nec}$ yields masks with Fid$^-$@10 of \fidMNofidelitymask, three times worse.

\paragraph{Necessity} Removing the ten features selected by SEAL lowers the predicted-class probability by only \fidPmask{} (Fid$^+$@10). This is a clear weakness. Integrated Gradients (\fidPig) and in particular GNNExplainer (\fidPgnnexplainer) identify features whose removal changes the prediction much more. The Elliptic features are highly redundant (72 of them aggregate the local features of neighbours), and a model can often recover the same information from features outside a ten-feature explanation. The necessity term constrains the complement of the whole hard view, including the edge view, and it is not sufficient to make the top ten features individually indispensable. GNNExplainer's high scores come at a price: it takes \fidTimegnnexplainer{} ms per transaction, about 300 times more than reading SEAL's masks (\fidTimemask{} ms), and its explanations are unstable. Its top-10 features have a Jaccard similarity of only \stabgnnexplainer{} under a small input perturbation, against \stabmask{} for SEAL. Integrated Gradients offers a middle ground at \fidTimeig{} ms.

\paragraph{Class-wise behaviour and agreement across models} Table~\ref{tab:explanations_class} shows that SEAL's sufficiency is similar for illicit and licit transactions, whereas Integrated Gradients and SEFraud's masks are much more sufficient for one class than for the other. Masks of independently trained RTXGNN models agree only weakly on the top-10 features (mean Jaccard similarity \crossSeedSeal, against \crossSeedRandom{} for random rankings), which again reflects the redundancy of the features. An analyst should therefore read an explanation as the features the \emph{deployed} model relied on, not as a model-independent property of the transaction.

In summary, SEAL provides explanations that are sufficient, stable and essentially free at inference time, but weakly necessary. For the transactions an analyst actually investigates, a more expensive post-hoc explainer can be run in addition when a stronger counterfactual statement is needed.

The synthetic benchmark isolates the two signals that Elliptic cannot provide: laundering rings whose members have no distinguishing features, and decoy cycles that can only be told apart by the timing of their hops (Table~\ref{tab:synthetic}). As intended, the task is hard. Feature-only models reach F1 below 0.5 in the default setting, since the features carry only indirect information (ring members have two extra transactions). Graph models improve on this.

The temporal mechanism helps where timing matters. In the default setting, RTXGNN (F1 \syndefaultrtxgnn) outperforms its variants without HRAPE (\syndefaultrtxgnnnohrape) and with Time2Vec on the same time gaps (\syndefaultrtxgnntimeTwovec). TGAT, whose time encoding is applied to every message, is the strongest model in this setting (\syndefaulttgat). When decoys are absent, so that the cycle structure alone identifies the rings, RTXGNN and its variant without SEAL masks are the best models (\synnodecoyrtxgnn). The 5\% label-noise setting is hard for all models (F1 about 0.4). The wide-burst setting leaves every static model unchanged, because only transaction times differ, which confirms that the static baselines cannot see timing.

\paragraph{Ground-truth evaluation of edge explanations} Because the planted ring transactions are known, the synthetic data allow a direct test of the edge explanations (Table~\ref{tab:synthetic_edges}). For each correctly detected ring member, we rank its incoming transactions by SEAL's edge importance $I_{uv}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:explanation}) and compute the AUC for identifying its two ring transactions. SEAL's importances reach an AUC of \edgeAucdefaultrtxgnnseal{} in the default setting and \edgeAucwideburstrtxgnnseal{} in the wide-burst setting. This is higher than GNNExplainer applied to the same models (\edgeAucdefaultrtxgnngnnexplainer{} and \edgeAucwideburstrtxgnngnnexplainer), than the raw attention weights (\edgeAucdefaultrtxgnnattention{} and \edgeAucwideburstrtxgnnattention), and than random scores (about 0.5). Over all \ringNdet{} ring members detected by the seed-0 model, the most important incoming transaction is a ring transaction in \ringHitPct\% of the cases. Figure~\ref{fig:ring} shows one ring. All its hops happen within two days, they receive most of the importance of their receiving accounts, and the ordinary transactions of the same accounts receive almost none. The variation of the edge AUC across graphs is large (standard deviations of about 0.2--0.3), so these results show a clear tendency rather than a uniform effect.
